# Networking Outreach

Use this skill for short outreach notes, warm-introduction follow-ups, relationship-building emails, and lightweight networking messages.

Related files:
- `Email-Drafting.md`
- `../../00-core/02-Communication-Style.md`

## Purpose

Write outreach that sounds like a real person making a specific, low-pressure connection. Avoid pitches, personal-brand statements, and disguised requests for market research.

## Context Check

Before drafting:

- Identify the recipient, relationship, and real reason for contact.
- Confirm whether the goal is a conversation, introduction, referral, future connection, or simple follow-up.
- Find one concrete point of relevance.
- Check whether a warm introduction or prior interaction changes the expected tone.
- Do not invent familiarity, admiration, shared interests, or learning goals.

## Default Shape

Use this structure unless the relationship clearly calls for something else:

1. Simple greeting.
2. One concrete reason for reaching out.
3. One short sentence of relevant context, only if needed.
4. Plain ask or connection intent.
5. Clean close.

## Core Rules

- Keep first outreach short, usually three to five sentences.
- Lead with the real purpose of the note.
- Prefer one concrete point of relevance over a broad background summary.
- If the goal is future relationship-building, say that plainly.
- Do not pretend to ask for advice, insight, or market perspective when the real goal is to make a useful connection.
- Avoid broad market-curiosity language unless that discussion is genuinely desired.
- Do not summarize a resume unless the recipient needs that context.
- Use admiration language only when it is specific and true.
- Avoid defensive disclaimers about what the sender is not asking for unless necessary.
- Make the next step easy to accept or ignore without pressure.
- Do not let `professional` drift into formal, watered-down, or generic business-email language.
- Prefer plain openings over filler like `I wanted to reach out` or `I hope this finds you well`.
- If a sentence sounds like it came from a recruiting template, a sales cadence, or a networking playbook, rewrite it in normal human language.
- Keep the note slightly specific rather than broadly polished.

## Useful Patterns

### Future Connection

Use when the honest goal is to get on someone's radar without pretending there is a deeper ask.

```text
Hi [Name] -

I have some relevant experience in [domain or context] and wanted to make a connection for the future. [Company, work, or context] caught my attention because [specific reason].

If useful, I would be glad to compare notes sometime.
```

### Specific Operator Connection

Use when the recipient has built, led, or operated something genuinely relevant.

```text
Hi [Name] -

I noticed your work on [specific company, product, or problem]. I have spent time around [relevant operating context], and the overlap seemed worth a short introduction.

I would be glad to connect if useful.
```

### Warm Introduction Follow-Up

Use after someone else has already made the connection.

```text
Hi [Name] -

Good to meet you, and thanks to [Introducer] for connecting us. My background includes [one relevant fact], and [specific company, problem, or context] seems close enough that it would be useful to know each other.

Happy to find a short time, or simply stay connected for now.
```

## Rewrite Checks

Before sending, check whether the draft:

- Says the real reason for contact.
- Contains only one or two relevant facts about the sender.
- Avoids fake learning questions.
- Avoids broad praise and abstract market language.
- Can be read quickly by a busy operator.
- Still sounds like something the sender would actually send, not a cleaned-up professional approximation.

If the note sounds bland after removing jargon, sharpen the concrete point of relevance. Do not add polish, adjectives, or extra background.

## Quality Bar

A strong outreach note:

- Is honest about its purpose.
- Gives the recipient enough context without a mini biography.
- Makes a modest, direct ask.
- Sounds specific to the recipient.
- Creates no false urgency or social pressure.

## Common Failure Modes

- Turning outreach into a mini-pitch.
- Asking fake learning questions to justify a meeting.
- Using vague praise instead of a concrete reason for contact.
- Hiding the ask behind excessive context.
- Making the recipient decode why the connection could be useful.
